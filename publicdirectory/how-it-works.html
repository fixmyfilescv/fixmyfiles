How FixMyFiles works: right in your browser

Most online converters upload your file to their servers to work on it. FixMyFiles does the work inside your browser tab instead, so the file never leaves your device. Here is what happens, in plain words — and how to check it yourself.

A typical online converter

Upload, wait, download

  1. Your file travels to their server. The whole video — every second of it — is sent over the internet before anything happens to it.
  2. It waits its turn. Your job joins everyone else’s on the service’s machines, and a busy hour or a free-tier limit slows it down.
  3. A copy sits on their disk. It is kept until their policy says to delete it. You have to trust that it is, and that nobody looks at it first.
  4. You download it back. The result comes back over the same connection, so a slow or metered line pays twice.

The file has left your hands, and the speed depends on your upload connection.

FixMyFiles

Open, fix, save — on your device

  1. Your file is opened in this tab. It is read from your disk into the browser’s memory, like a video player opening it. No connection is made to send it.
  2. Your device does the work. Your browser’s own codecs — often your graphics chip’s video encoder — or a WebAssembly engine such as FFmpeg, with no queue.
  3. Nothing is kept anywhere. There is no server copy to delete. The only copy is the one in this tab’s memory, gone when you close it.
  4. You save it straight away. The finished file is handed to your browser as a download: no round trip, however slow your connection.

The file never leaves your device, and the speed depends on your device.

The pipeline

  1. 1

    Read. When you add a file, the page reads its bytes with the File API. The file stays on your disk; the tab holds a copy in memory.

  2. 2

    Inspect. The tool checks what it’s working with: the real format, pixel size, codecs, length and sound track — what the size meter and the presets need.

  3. 3

    Process. The engine for the job runs — in a Web Worker where the work is heavy, so the page stays responsive and Cancel always works.

  4. 4

    Save. The result is built as a Blob in memory and offered as a normal download, one by one or as a ZIP. Closing the tab discards everything.

What follows from the design

Built on four privacy principles

  • Nothing to retain

    We cannot keep, look at or share your files, because they never reach us. There is no upload endpoint on the site at all.

  • Works offline

    A service worker keeps the pages and engines you’ve used, so a tool you’ve opened once works with no connection at all.

    Using it offline

  • No accounts

    No email address, no password, no card. You arrive, fix the file and leave; there is nothing to sign up for.

  • Check it yourself

    Open your browser’s Network tab while a tool runs and watch: the page downloads its code, and sends none of your file.

    How to check

Why a Web Worker?

JavaScript on a page runs on one thread, shared with scrolling and clicks. Encoding a batch of AVIF images or running FFmpeg over a long video can take seconds to minutes of solid computation, so that work runs in a Web Worker — a second thread — and reports progress back. The page never freezes, and the Cancel button always works.

What “0 bytes uploaded” means precisely

The page makes network requests for its own code and, the first time, for engines like FFmpeg. Those are downloads. The privacy meter in the header wraps every way the page can send data — fetch, XMLHttpRequest, sendBeacon and WebSockets — and counts the bytes that leave; requests from its background workers are reported through the service worker. For your files, it stays at 0. Check it yourself.

Questions

Which browsers does it work in?

Current Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari, on desktop and mobile. The tools need JavaScript and WebAssembly, which every browser from the last five years supports. The fastest video path uses WebCodecs; where a browser lacks it, the tool falls back to FFmpeg and tells you.

Why is the first run of some tools slower?

A few formats need an engine the first time: FFmpeg (a one-time 31 MB download) for older video and audio formats such as AVI, WMV and WMA, WebAssembly encoders for AVIF, WebP and MozJPEG, and the text reader and a language model (about 5 MB together) for Image to Text. After that they load from your browser’s cache, offline included.

How big a file can it handle?

As big as your device’s memory allows. A laptop handles videos of several gigabytes; phones have less room, so very long 4K footage is better done on a computer. Nothing is refused for being too big.

Can I use it for passport scans, family videos or unreleased music?

That is what it is built for: the file is never sent anywhere, so there is no copy on someone else’s server to leak or be requested. If your employer has rules about which software may open client files, those rules still apply — the difference here is that nothing leaves the device.

If nothing is uploaded, how is the site paid for?

The site is static files, and your device does the work, so it costs very little to run. There are no ads and nothing is sold; it is paid for by the person who runs it. If that ever changes, the About page will say so plainly.

Is the code open source?

The libraries doing the work all are, and the licenses page lists each one with what it does here.
